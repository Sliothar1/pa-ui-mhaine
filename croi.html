Croí Uí Mhaine · The heartland

Croí Uí Mhaine · The heartland

A few fields, a lake, a friary and some old forts in east Galway: the home ground of the Uí Cheallaigh lords and the poets they fed.

An talamh · The land

Stand on the road in Fothannach (Fohenagh) on a still evening and you are in the middle of it. To the south is Cill Chonaill, Kilconnell, where the friars built their house beside Callow Lough in 1353 under Ó Ceallaigh patronage. North and west lie Gallagh, the castle country now called Castleblakeney, Tycooly and Caltra. In between are low green ridges, bog and seasonal lakes, and the townlands whose names still remember who lived here.

This was the lordship of Uí Mhaine at its heart in the later Middle Ages: a world of cattle and hospitality, of a lord in his pailís (palace enclosure) and the learned families settled on good land around him, paid in land for their poems, music and law.

Pailís agus Dún · Pallas and Doon

Elizabeth FitzPatrick (Landscapes of the Learned, 2023, pp. 147–149) identified this landscape at Pallas, in Fohenagh, as the Ó Ceallaigh court, with An Lios Mór as the lord's seat. She places the famous Christmas feast of 1351, when Uilliam Buide Ó Ceallaigh welcomed the poets of Ireland, at Doon Fort: An Dún, in Doon Upper, a raised oval platform looking down over Loch na nÉigeas, "the lake of the poets", known today as Boggaun Lough.

The full story of that feast is on The court page.

Source: FitzPatrick, Landscapes of the Learned (Oxford University Press, 2023), pp. 147–149, in our own words.

Mar a insítear go háitiúil · As told locally

Ask around Fohenagh and you will hear the heartland told townland by townland. Ballydoogan, Baile Uí Dhúgáin, is remembered as the home of the poets, the Ó Dubhagáin learned family whose lands FitzPatrick also places beside the court. Next door is Ballynabanaba, which the Ordnance Survey's John O'Donovan wrote down as Baile na Banba; Banba is one of the old poetic names for Ireland itself. Then there is Doon, An Dún, "the fort", and a little ridge that runs down through the fields with an old fort sitting on it.

A few miles north-west, Ballymacward, Baile Mhic an Bhaird, carries the name of the Mac an Bhaird, a family of hereditary poets. Bees are kept there now, and you can taste the heartland in PA Honey.

As told locally (Garry Lohan). Irish forms: Placenames Database of Ireland (logainm.ie) and O'Donovan's Ordnance Survey Name Books via Galway County Library's People and Places of Galway.

Logainmneacha an chroí · Townland names of the heartland

Some of these are not pinned on our map yet; they are listed by name only.

  • Ballydoogan · Baile Uí Dhúgáin: Ó Dúgáin's (Ó Dubhagáin's) townland.
  • Cartrondoogan · Cartrún Uí Dhúgáin: Ó Dúgáin's cartron. A cartrún is a land-division unit; it sits between Ballydoogan and Doon Upper.
  • Ballynabanaba · Baile na Banaba (O'Donovan: Baile na Banba, "town of Banba").
  • Doon Upper · An Dún: "the fort".
  • Lisnascreena · Lios na Scríne: "fort of the shrine".
  • Lisloughlin · Lios Maoileachlainn: Maoileachlainn's fort (in Ballymacward parish; locally Lislochlin).
  • Clonbrock · Cluain Broc: "meadow of the badgers".
  • Ballymacward · Baile Mhic an Bhaird: the town of Mac an Bhaird, the bard's family.

Official Irish forms from the Placenames Database of Ireland (logainm.ie) as listed on townlands.ie; translations follow O'Donovan's Name Books (Galway County Library, People and Places of Galway). Spellings are the official English forms.

An líne bheo · The living line

The story did not end with the lordship. In 1767 Empress Maria Theresa made Festus O'Kelly a Count of the Holy Roman Empire, Count of Gallagh and Tycooly, a title granted in perpetuity to his descendants. The O'Kelly family line continues today, and the old names of the heartland are still theirs and ours.

Source: Dictionary of Irish Biography, "O'Kelly de Gallagh et Tycooly".
