Nollaig 1351 · Christmas 1351

Cúirt Uí Cheallaigh i bhFothnach

The O'Kelly court at Fohenagh, and the Christmas when the poets of Ireland came to one house.

Filidh Éireann go haointeach · The poets of Ireland to one house

At Christmas 1351, Uilliam Buide Ó Ceallaigh (William Boy O'Kelly), lord of Uí Mhaine, sent word across Ireland: every poet, judge, historian and musician was welcome at his house for the feast. They came in such numbers that the gathering was remembered for centuries.

The poet Gofraidh Fionn Ó Dálaigh, a leading master of the bardic schools of Munster, marked the occasion in a long praise poem whose opening line gives it its name, roughly "the poets of Ireland go to one house". In it he pictures the guests arriving, the generosity of the host, and the rows of new dwellings put up so that each art had its own place. It made Uilliam's hospitality famous, and the Annals of Clonmacnoise echo its first verse.

Source: Eleanor Knott (ed. and trans.), "Filidh Éireann go haointeach: William Ó Ceallaigh's Christmas feast to the poets of Ireland, A.D. 1351", Ériu 5 (1911), 50–69; Bardic Poetry Database, poem 932; Dictionary of Irish Biography. Mícheál Hoyne has since argued the feast was also a show of support for a profession under pressure.

Where: the feast was held at the court of An Lios Mór, Pallas, in Fohenagh, as Elizabeth FitzPatrick shows in Landscapes of the Learned.

Bealach na bhFilí · The poets' route (as told locally)

Local tradition tells how the poets came: west out of Ballinasloe along the Eiscir Riada, the great esker road, with the Turoe Stone away to the south on their left. At Kilconnell they turned north, then went on through Doon and Gallagh to the court at An Lios Mór, Pallas, in Fohenagh. You can follow a reconstruction on the time map (Routes layer).

Compare: Cathair Mhic Neachtain, Cahermacnaghten

In the Burren, Co. Clare, the stone cashel of Cahermacnaghten was home to the Uí Dhábhoireann (O'Davorens), a family of lawyers who ran a law school there. It shows what a learned family's place looked like: a walled homestead, a house for the master, room for students, and good land granted by the lord in return for their skill. The harpers and poets around Pallas would have lived on similar terms, close to their patron and honoured for their art.

Source: FitzPatrick, Landscapes of the Learned (comparison, in our own words).
