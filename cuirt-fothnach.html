Nollaig 1351 · Christmas 1351

Cúirt Uí Cheallaigh i bhFothnach

The O'Kelly court at Fohenagh, and the Christmas when the poets of Ireland came to one house.

Filidh Éireann go haointeach · The poets of Ireland to one house

At Christmas 1351, Uilliam Buide Ó Ceallaigh (William Boy O'Kelly), lord of Uí Mhaine, sent word across Ireland: every poet, judge, historian and musician was welcome at his house for the feast. They came in such numbers that the gathering was remembered for centuries.

The poet Gofraidh Fionn Ó Dálaigh, a leading master of the bardic schools of Munster, marked the occasion in a long praise poem whose opening line gives it its name, roughly "the poets of Ireland go to one house". In it he pictures the guests arriving, the generosity of the host, and the rows of new dwellings put up so that each art had its own place. It made Uilliam's hospitality famous, and the Annals of Clonmacnoise echo its first verse (Dictionary of Irish Biography, "Ó Dálaigh, Gofraidh Fionn").

Source: Eleanor Knott (ed. and trans.), "Filidh Éireann go haointeach: William Ó Ceallaigh's Christmas feast to the poets of Ireland, A.D. 1351", Ériu 5 (1911), 50–69; Bardic Poetry Database, poem 932; Dictionary of Irish Biography.

Where: the feast was held at An Dún, Doon Fort, in the townland of Doon Upper, Fohenagh. The poem describes a hall of stone and timber put up for the occasion on the edge of a lake called Loch na nÉigeas, "the lake of the poets", and likens it to Emain Macha, the ancient royal seat of Ulster. Elizabeth FitzPatrick's 2016 field survey, published in Landscapes of the Learned (2023), identifies that place as Doon Fort: a raised oval platform about 60 by 50 metres with a steep edge, set between the lands of the Ó Dubhagáin and Ó Longargáin families and looking south-west over a seasonal lake now called Boggaun Lough. It sits in the same court landscape as the lord's pailís at An Lios Mór, Pallas (FitzPatrick 2023, pp. 147–149; Knott, Ériu 5, 60–61). The place kept its importance: a castle of Tadhg Ó Ceallaigh stood there in 1574, though no walls remained by 1838, and Doon House later took its place.

Bealach na bhFilí · The poets' route (as told locally)

Local tradition tells how the poets came: west out of Ballinasloe along the Eiscir Riada, the great esker road, with the Turoe Stone away to the south-west on their left. At Kilconnell they turned north by Callow Lough, and came to Doon Fort above Loch na nÉigeas, the feast hall, close to the lord's court at An Lios Mór, Pallas, in Fohenagh. Compare: Cathair Mhic Neachtain, Cahermacnaghten

In the Burren, Co. Clare, the stone cashel of Cahermacnaghten was home to the Uí Dhábhoireann (O'Davorens), a family of lawyers who ran a law school there. It shows what a learned family's place looked like: a walled homestead, a house for the master, room for students, and good land granted by the lord in return for their skill. The harpers and poets around Pallas would have lived on similar terms, close to their patron and honoured for their art.
